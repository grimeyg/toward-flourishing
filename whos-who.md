# Who's who

Every person cited in this doc, with what each contributes to the framework. Opponents, the views the framework argues against, are marked.

## Start with these twelve

1. **Plato:** the just city and the just soul, the framework's two scales.
2. **Aristotle:** virtue as a mean, reworked here as the mean between security and flourishing.
3. **Charles Peirce:** the three paths, as tenacity, authority, and science.
4. **John Rawls:** the veil of ignorance, the reason the floor covers everyone.
5. **H.L.A. Hart:** why any society needs a floor at all.
6. **Amartya Sen:** capabilities versus functionings; the faster and the starving.
7. **Martha Nussbaum:** the capabilities list, the floor's lineage.
8. **Derk Pereboom:** no basic desert; responsibility looks forward.
9. **Daniel Dennett:** freedom by degrees, and the self as a story.
10. **Philippa Foot:** morality as a system of hypothetical imperatives (1972).
11. **P.F. Strawson:** reactive attitudes, the main objection to the analysis of "good."
12. **Peter Geach:** "good" needs a noun, the ancestor of the analysis of "good."

## Method and foundations

- **Plato**, *Republic*: city and soul at two scales. Thrasymachus is the consistent egoist whom Socrates only silences. The Form of the Good is the bare verdict treated as a property (opponent there).
- **Aristotle**: virtue as a mean "relative to us," the bent stick for correcting toward it, a final end chosen for its own sake, and living well as an activity complete at each moment (*Metaphysics* Θ.6). Also "good" said in many ways, and the knowledge condition on voluntary action.
- **Charles Peirce**, "The Fixation of Belief" (1877): tenacity, authority, and science match felt certainty, conformity, and the virtuous path; his a priori method is conformity to the past.
- **Rudolf Carnap**: explication, replacing a vague concept with a precise one that keeps its core uses. The framework's method.
- **Ludwig Wittgenstein**: family resemblance, how compressed concepts hang together. His "Lecture on Ethics" separates relative from absolute good.
- **John Rawls**: the veil of ignorance, the difference principle, the priority of the right over the good, reflective equilibrium, just savings, and the "natural lottery." Later, principles acceptable to reasonable citizens rather than proven true.
- **Derek Parfit**, *Reasons and Persons* (1984): pure egoism defeats itself; the leveling-down objection to valuing fairness alone; prioritarianism; personal identity by degrees.
- **Ralph Waldo Emerson**: "foolish consistency," refusing to change one's mind, is felt certainty.
- **Karl Popper**: piecemeal social engineering, a society correcting in small doses.
- **Daniel Kahneman and Gary Klein**: instinct is reliable only when trained on a stable world with quick feedback.

## The axioms: causation, emergence, and mind

- **John Conway**: the Game of Life, simple rules building complex patterns.
- **Mark Bedau**, "Weak Emergence" (1997): patterns derivable only by running the simulation.
- **Stephen Wolfram**: computational irreducibility, your working definition of emergence.
- **David Chalmers** (opponent): the hard problem and the zombie; consciousness as strong emergence.
- **Hilary Putnam**: functionalism, mind as software.
- **Keith Frankish**: illusionism, the alternative to your realist functionalism.
- **Daniel Dennett**: freedom by degrees built from the Game of Life (*Freedom Evolves*), and the self as a "center of narrative gravity."
- **Harry Frankfurt**: second-order desires. Unbidden Thoughts defeats ultimate responsibility, not his account.
- **Arthur Danto, Hans-Georg Gadamer, T.S. Eliot**: why meaning stays two-way across time. Danto: some true descriptions can't be known when events happen. Gadamer: a text's meaning develops through interpretation. Eliot: new works rearrange old ones.

## Interaction, power, and education

- **Donald Horton and Richard Wohl**: parasocial interaction, a relation that never pushes back.
- **Adam Galinsky**: a brief feeling of power makes people worse at taking others' perspective.
- **Lord Acton**: power "tends to corrupt."
- **Francis Bacon**: "knowledge is power."
- **John Dewey**, *Experience and Education* (1938): interaction as a principle of education.
- **Paulo Freire**, *Pedagogy of the Oppressed* (1968): against the banking model; the teacher learns too.
- **Ziva Kunda**: motivated reasoning, interaction turned into entrenchment.

## Ontology: functions

- **John Searle**: assigned and status functions ("X counts as Y in context C"). He also made "direction of fit" a standard term.
- **Robert Cummins**: systemic functions, the rock that splits the stream.
- **Larry Wright**: a function explains why a thing is there.
- **Ruth Millikan**: functions from selection history.
- **J.J. Gibson**: affordances, what things allow before anyone assigns a use.

## The five states, meaning, and the self

- **Elizabeth Anscombe**, *Intention* (1957): direction of fit; the shopper's list and the detective's record.
- **Judith Butler**: precariousness, shared by all bodies, versus precarity, unevenly distributed.
- **Martin Hägglund**, *This Life*: we care about things because they can be lost.
- **Frank Martela and Michael Steger**: meaning's three strands, coherence, purpose, and significance.
- **Dan McAdams**: narrative identity and redemption sequences.
- **Sigmund Freud**, "On Transience" (1916): mourning in advance, the flourishing-side vice of attachment.
- **Viktor Frankl**: meaning in suffering; the March 30 prisoner shows meaning needs a floor.
- **Primo Levi**, *The Drowned and the Saved*: survival owed much to luck; the gray zone.
- **James Stockdale**: the Stockdale paradox, brutal facts with unbroken faith.
- **Albert Camus**: Sisyphus, lucidity without inventing a point.
- **Bernard Williams**: ground projects; "one thought too many" on partiality; internal reasons.
- **Jennifer Campbell**: self-concept clarity, for the open question on depression.
- **Hesiod**: timed planting by the Pleiades, myth and prediction intertwined.

## Freedom and responsibility

- **Derk Pereboom**, *Living Without Free Will* (2001): hard incompatibilism; no basic desert, and responsibility looks forward.
- **Gregg Caruso**: with Pereboom, the public health–quarantine model of punishment.
- **John Martin Fischer and Mark Ravizza**: reasons-responsiveness, the framework's ownership.
- **John Locke**: liberty as a power; a social contract floor of life, liberty, and property.
- **Sendhil Mullainathan and Eldar Shafir**, *Scarcity* (2013): precarity's tax on mental bandwidth.

## Virtue, needs, and striving

- **Marsha Linehan**: opposite action, from dialectical behavior therapy.
- **Arthur Schopenhauer**: life swings between unmet desire and boredom; projects answer him.
- **Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi**: flow between anxiety and boredom, the same shape as the mean.
- **Bernard Suits**, *The Grasshopper*: a game is the voluntary attempt to overcome unnecessary obstacles.
- **Martin Seligman**: learned helplessness, friction that affords no purchase.
- **Friedrich Nietzsche**: "what does not kill me makes me stronger," false as stated.
- **Epicurus**: tranquility, read here as a byproduct of flourishing.
- **John Stuart Mill**: "better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied," against blind living.
- **Todd Kashdan and Michael Steger**: curiosity predicts meaning and well-being, the outward sign of flourishing.
- **Peter Sterling and Joseph Eyer**: allostasis, stability through change.
- **C.S. Holling**: ecological resilience, the forest that burns and regrows.
- **Hans Jonas**: obligations to future generations.
- **Joseph Henrich**, *The Secret of Our Success*: culture carries what humans learn about living together.
- **April Bleske-Rechek**: people are less willing to sacrifice kin in trolley dilemmas.

## Ethics and the uses of "good"

- **Philippa Foot**: "Morality as a System of Hypothetical Imperatives" (1972), which the framework keeps, and *Natural Goodness* (2001), which it declines.
- **Immanuel Kant** (opponent): the categorical imperative.
- **Peter Geach**, "Good and Evil" (1956): "good" works like "big" and needs a noun.
- **Judith Jarvis Thomson**, *Normativity* (2008): all goodness is goodness in a way.
- **Georg Henrik von Wright**, *The Varieties of Goodness* (1963): sorts "good" much as your chart does.
- **G.E. Moore** (opponent): good as a non-natural property.
- **A.J. Ayer, Simon Blackburn, Allan Gibbard**: expressivism, verdicts as cheers.
- **Michael Ridge and Daniel Boisvert**: hybrid theories, verdicts as both claim and cheer.
- **J.L. Austin**: speech acts, behind the performative uses of "good."
- **Christine Korsgaard**, "Two Distinctions in Goodness" (1983): justice valued as an end, with its value coming from relations.
- **Claudia Mueller and Carol Dweck**: praise for intelligence makes children avoid hard tasks.
- **June Tangney**: guilt prompts repair; shame prompts hiding.
- **P.F. Strawson**, "Freedom and Resentment" (1962): reactive attitudes respond to goodwill, so resenting a wrong already holds others to a standard.
- **Jonathan Shay**: moral injury, the damage of violating one's own code.

## Grounding: why aim goods at strangers

- **David Hume**: the sensible knave, governed rather than argued away.
- **Alan Gewirth**, *Reason and Morality* (1978): every agent claims freedom and well-being, so must grant them to others.
- **Robert Axelrod**: tit-for-tat; cooperation wins repeated games.
- **Robert Trivers**: reciprocal altruism.
- **Richard Dawkins**: concern for strangers as a "blessed" misfiring.
- **Alasdair MacIntyre**, *Dependent Rational Animals* (1999): ethics can't ignore biology.
- **Terence Cuneo** (opponent): companions in guilt; if epistemic norms are categorical, why not moral ones?
- **Larry Laudan**: rules of method as hypothetical imperatives, the framework's answer to Cuneo.
- **Thomas Kelly**: the main critique of that answer.

## Justice and the floor

- **Amartya Sen**, *Development as Freedom* (1999): capabilities versus functionings, the faster and the starving, and no famine in a functioning democracy.
- **Martha Nussbaum**: the capabilities list, internal and combined capabilities, the emotions capability, and *The Fragility of Goodness* on luck and loss.
- **H.L.A. Hart**, *The Concept of Law* (1961): the minimum content of natural law, why a floor at all.
- **Thomas Hobbes**: the contract's first floor, protection from violent death; a promise not to resist being killed is void.
- **Robert Nozick** (opponent): taxing earnings as forced labor. His experience machine also appears, against blind living.
- **Michael Walzer**: dirty hands, the moral residue of justified injustice.
- **Jon Elster**, *Ulysses and the Sirens* (1979): precommitment; constitutions as Odysseus at the mast.
- **Pamela Herd and Donald Moynihan**: administrative burden, the cost of claiming help.
- **Matthew Lipman**: Philosophy for Children, a precedent for the education item.
- **Peter Singer**: the expanding circle.
