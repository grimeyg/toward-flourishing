# Questions

Open questions first, then the ones already resolved, with their answers.

## Open

- [ ] **How should the floor be measured, so each item is specific enough to claim?** The floor lists what a just society guarantees: health care, food, housing, community, education, physical safety, and harm reduction. As written, each names a direction rather than a threshold, so no one can point to the moment it's missing. A guarantee is claimable when a person can show it's unmet. Some measures exist: food security has standard survey scales, housing is often called unaffordable when it takes more than 30% of income, and the UN's Multidimensional Poverty Index measures several items together. Nussbaum leaves thresholds to each society's political process. Open: whether to fix thresholds like these, or keep items revisable through the test.
- [ ] **What form should rituals of acknowledgment take, and at what scale?** Taxing wealth to fund the floor is a justified injustice, and it leaves a moral residue: a real loss that only the person who bore it can judge. [The framework](framework.md) proposes rituals that tell those taxed what their loss secured, with the liturgies of Athens as a precedent. Open: the form, such as a personal statement of what one's taxes funded (Britain has sent annual tax summaries since 2014), public honor, or stories from those served; and the scale, from a city to a nation. Whatever the form, the cautions in [the framework](framework.md) apply: gratitude can't be required, or it becomes the orange cell.
- [ ] **How much animal suffering is pointless, and what would reducing it cost the human floor?** The floor can extend to animals, since the Golden Rule rests on suffering and many animals can suffer, but human floors come first. Two things are unsettled. First, pointlessness: the framework lets only the sufferer judge whether their suffering has a point, and animals can't tell us, so the judgment has to be made from outside, by reading behavior as with anyone who can't state their needs. Second, cost: reducing animal suffering, as in factory farming, can raise food prices, which presses on the food item of the human floor. [The framework](framework.md) says to avoid it "where it costs little"; how little is the open part.
- [ ] **Which compressions get the "good" treatment next?** The uses of "good" gives that word the full treatment: it maps the word's uses by grammatical form (good at, for, as, or a bare verdict) and by the end each serves, which shows where standards hide. The Compression axiom names other words that bundle loosely related ideas the same way. Free will is partly done, since Freedom as a matter of degree rejects the word and keeps "freedom," and time looks settled under Compression. Justice and meaning are the main candidates left; beauty and purpose are also on the Compression list.
- [ ] **Does an unsettled self-concept feed depression?** Relational identity says the self-story is tested against how one acts with people and in situations. If isolation cuts off that test, the story may drift from the behavior and become unsettled, and an early draft proposed that this feeds depression. That's a hypothesis, not a finding. Research on self-concept clarity, how clearly and consistently people define themselves (Jennifer Campbell and colleagues), is the place to test it, along with research on loneliness.
- [ ] **What is a relation?** Relational identity needs relations that push back, and the doc already allows relations with situations and environments, not only people. Parasocial ties and broadcast media show the edges. Candidate dimensions: whether each side pushes back on the other, whether each side is aware of the other, how wide the channel back is (a voice or a number), and whether it is ongoing or one-shot. A starting definition for the framework: ongoing contact with something that pushes back.
- [ ] **How far does self-preference reach?** Since loved ones are part of the self, partiality is justified exactly as far as self-preference is. Two cases differ. Refusing to sacrifice oneself, or a loved one, is permitted, since sacrifice is beyond duty. Redirecting harm onto innocent bystanders is harder: self-defense permits harming an attacker, but the five on the track aren't attacking anyone, and the law generally won't accept necessity as a defense for killing an innocent to save oneself (*R v Dudley and Stephens*, 1884). Saving one's own at their expense may be excusable, understandable and not blamed, rather than justified.

  **Drawing lots.** When someone must bear unavoidable harm, lots offer a separate path. They don't avoid the violence, but they remove bias from the selection, which matters because in a lifeboat everyone doing the calculating is also a candidate. They can also make the violence smaller: if the loser complies there's no struggle, and the survivors carry less self-damage because none of them chose. *United States v. Holmes* (1842) endorsed lots as the fair method. But the loser keeps the right to self-defense, and Hobbes held that a promise not to resist being killed is void, so lots work only while the loser keeps consenting. Some have: after the whaleship *Essex* sank in 1820, the young sailor who drew the short lot accepted it. Most people probably wouldn't, and if the loser refuses, violence is necessary again and the calculation applies.
- [ ] **Which functional organizations can suffer?** Functionalism gives a criterion in principle: the right organization suffers, whatever it's made of. Analogy settles the clear cases, since mammals and birds share our behavior and nervous systems (Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness, 2012; New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness, 2024). It runs out at the edges: insects, where sentience is uncertain, and minds in silicon, where behavior and biology come apart. Open: what counts as evidence of suffering when analogy fails.
- [ ] **What carries the floor past what society needs?** Societies have stayed stable while torturing outsiders, enslaving people, and harming animals, so "what makes society possible" covers less than the floor. Candidate: citizens' near-universal aversion to suffering, applied consistently. Open: whether consistency alone extends the floor to outsiders and animals.
- [ ] **Who owes the floor across borders?** The floor is mostly each society's job, since it reduces precarity for the people who live there. But a society may truly lack room or means for something, as when land is scarce. Open: when the duty can pass to another society that has room, and who pays for the move, since moving it often shifts the cost onto the people the floor protects (see A right with nowhere to use it, under The floor in society).
- [ ] **Is anti-conformity a fourth path?** Reflexively opposing the group looks like a fourth way to meet the world's pushback, but it may be conformity reversed. Richard Willis separated three responses to group pressure: conformity, anticonformity, and independence ("Conformity, Independence, and Anticonformity," 1965). The anticonformist depends on the group as much as the conformist does, only in reverse, so the world's check is still dodged. The motive differs: conformity seeks belonging, while anticonformity seeks distinctiveness or pushes back against the pressure itself, which Jack Brehm called reactance (1966). Its likely failure is a self defined by opposition. Independence, steered by neither the group nor its opposite, is close to the virtuous path. Open: whether anticonformity is a fourth path or conformity with its own motive, and where it belongs in the paths diagram. Paul Nail and colleagues' Double Diamond Model (2013), with two kinds of conformity and three of anticonformity, is a place to start.
- [ ] **Do the non-virtuous paths share a motive: protecting the self-model?** The paths are defined by what reflection checks against: the world, the self, or the group. That is the mechanism. The motive may be the same for both red paths: felt certainty protects the self-model, and conformity protects belonging. On this reading the ego is the mechanism that keeps a self-model stable, and only someone comfortable with being wrong can relax it. "Comfort" is the wrong word for what is guarded: William Swann's self-verification research (1983) found that people with negative self-views seek confirmation of them, so the thing protected is a predictable self-model, even a painful one. Ziva Kunda's motivated reasoning (1990) and Dan Kahan's identity-protective cognition describe the same pattern. Open: whether the motive belongs in the definition of the paths or stays a second layer, and whether the virtuous path is simply reflection that can afford to be wrong.
- [ ] **Can a system run the virtuous path when no one in it does?** A trial lawyer runs the red loop (see the [Causation diagram](axioms.md#causation)) on purpose: they build one stable story and defend it against whatever disagrees. That is felt certainty in form, but the story belongs to the client, not to the lawyer's self-model, and the role is taken on knowingly. The adversarial system is built around this. Two advocates each defend a story, a neutral judge or jury sits between them, and rules of evidence and cross-examination force both stories to meet the world. No single participant checks against the world, yet the structure does. Peer review and formal debate work the same way.
  - **Why lawyers are seen as unethical.** The public sees the motivated reasoning but not the structure that checks it. The suspicion is half right: the individual's reasoning is red even when the system is blue.
  - **Where it fails.** The role leaks into the self-model. A lawyer who starts believing the story, or whose self-model becomes "I'm someone who wins," is protecting an ego, not representing a client. That is when lines get crossed: hidden evidence, coached testimony, attacks on a witness's character in place of their account. A test: can the person drop the story when the case ends?
  - **The prosecutor is the test case.** With no client of their own to serve, a prosecutor must keep the blue loop running alone. The comment to the ABA's Model Rule 3.8 says a prosecutor "has the responsibility of a minister of justice and not simply that of an advocate."
  - **Prior work.** This is the debate over role morality. Charles Fried, "The Lawyer as Friend" (1976), defended partisan advocacy as a kind of friendship. Gerald Postema, "Moral Responsibility in Professional Ethics" (1980), and David Luban, *Lawyers and Justice* (1988), argued that a role cannot wash out personal moral responsibility.
  - **Open.** Whether the virtuous path is a property of individuals, of systems, or both. What a system needs to turn red loops into a blue one: a neutral arbiter, advocates on every side, shared rules of evidence. Whether unequal resources (an overloaded public defender against a well-funded firm) break the structure. And whether justice, as the facilitation of flourishing, is partly the design of such systems.

**Parked from The uses of "good"**

- [ ] **Is beauty an unconscious verdict?** "That's beautiful" names no standard, and the standard may be fit: many pairings compressed into one felt judgment. That would keep beauty in the pleasurable column and explain tragedy (pain in the content, beauty in the form).
- [ ] **Does verdict-ness rise left to right in every row?** Functional standards are mostly shared and observable (the engine runs), pleasurable ones rest on the judge's own experience (good with cheese), and judgments of goods aimed at others usually rest on a standard the judge supplies (good friend, good for the community). If so, one arrow above the chart's columns, from "standard shared" to "standard supplied by the judge," would show it, with the orange cell as the point where a judge-supplied standard lands on a whole person.

- [x] Is taking wealth an injustice? Yes, a justified one when necessary to secure the floor
- [x] When is an inhibition necessary? When there is literally no other option to get what one needs
- [x] Rename the virtue axis? Security vs flourishing
- [x] Where does justice fit? It is the aim the virtues serve
- [x] Needs for what? Security, meaning, and stimulation; see Needs and striving
- [x] Needs of those who can't tell us? Not fully knowable; read body language, facilitate comfort
- [x] Is flourishing judged across a whole life? No; it can't be completed
- [x] Is suffering failure to flourish? No; they come apart in both directions
- [x] The consistent fanatic? Only the sufferer can judge whether their own suffering has a point
- [x] Freedom? Options, power, knowledge, and ownership; see Freedom as a matter of degree
- [x] How to split the five states? By direction of fit; see The five universal states
- [x] Lever vs footbridge? Only immediacy differs; pushing is supererogatory
- [x] What does a death for honor achieve? It sacrifices individual flourishing; see Can a value outrank flourishing?, below
- [x] "Comfort" means two things? The path is now called felt certainty
- [x] A third path? Conformity, consistent only with others; the virtuous path sits between it and felt certainty
- [x] Where does the analysis of "good" belong? On its own page ([The uses of "good"](uses-of-good.md)), summarized under Compression in [Axioms](axioms.md#compression)
- [x] Does subjectivity always rise across the verdict row? No; what matters is who holds the standard relative to whose ends are at stake

* [x] Is precarity only what others impose? No; precarity comes from everything, natural and social. One word covers it; what matters is whether it can be reduced, and by which lever

- [x] Is long-term stability a value or only the test? A value: optional for individuals, who may trade it for other ends, but necessary for governments, because future citizens' flourishing depends on it. Resilience remains the test
- [x] Is Peirce's a priori method a fourth path? No; it is conformity to the past, instincts trained by evolution, upbringing, and culture, that feels like felt certainty. The floor still binds it; see Precedent in [the framework](framework.md)
- [x] Can a value outrank flourishing? No; a value flourishes only in the people who hold it, so dying for one is dying for others' flourishing, or for the integrity of one's own self-story (survival of meaning). Spreading through culture doesn't show a value is good: the floor still applies, and lasting doesn't show fit
- [x] Does the virtue diagram scale from persons to institutions and societies? In shape, not mechanism; see Virtue at scale
- [x] What do praise and blame become without basic desert? Signals about the process, not verdicts on the person. Aimed at the person, both push toward the security side: praise toward avoiding challenge, blame toward rumination or conformity. See the orange cell in The uses of "good"
- [x] Who applies the "by what standard?" test? Wrong question: the test is a call for everyone to make their standards explicit when they judge, praising and blaming the process rather than the person
- [x] Does self-consistency explain the equal-consequences intuition about justice (Plato's *Republic*, Book II)? Yes. The code one holds, not causing others the suffering one wouldn't want, supplies the content; keeping it even when outcomes are equal is what self-consistency means. Breaking it would change the chooser, so the consequences were never fully equal. This is close to Plato's own answer, justice as a soul not divided against itself, and fits Korsgaard's "Two Distinctions in Goodness" (1983): justice valued as an end, with its value coming from relations
- [x] Is the orange cell the only bare verdict on another mind? No; weak, loser, weird, and crazy also judge a whole person with the standard hidden, their standards aren't moral, and some, like crazy, also end the listening. Naming a domain isn't always naming a standard: "good at listening" is factual where its bar is shared and observable, a judgment where the speaker supplies it. See the orange cell in The uses of "good"
- [x] Does relational identity justify partiality, or only explain it? It justifies it: survival comes first, and the right to self-defense extends to family and friends, since they are part of oneself. Bernard Williams makes a similar case with "one thought too many" ("Persons, Character and Morality," in *Moral Luck*). How far it reaches is still open; see How far does self-preference reach?, above
- [x] Should the "good to" row keep only treatment? Yes; the infinitive uses (good to go, good to know, good to see you) are mostly stock phrases and moved to the performative-uses table
- [x] Is the moral column a direction rather than a kind? Yes; moral goods are any kind of good aimed at others, and fairness and honesty are functional goods of consistency, which serves flourishing. The chart now shows direction as a shaded band in each box
- [x] Does the Hypothetical Imperative fail against the amoralist? No, for this framework's purpose: it is for persons and governments, after Plato's city and soul. A government needs grounds to constrain the torturer, not to refute him: he violates what makes society possible. Hume's sensible knave is governed, not argued away, and Rawls asks only that principles be acceptable to reasonable citizens ("Justice as Fairness: Political not Metaphysical," 1985). In a person, the amoralist is an appetite, constrained by precommitment, the soul's version of law (Elster). Epistemic claims are conditional too: they diagram what happens off the virtuous path rather than command, which answers Cuneo's companions-in-guilt argument (Laudan's normative naturalism; Thomas Kelly, 2003, for the critique). Stability that permits change is necessary for flourishing, up to the mean, and never sufficient; resilience is not worth
- [x] Should the floor match Nussbaum's ten? No. The floor is already set: its negative half, rights against interference, covers political voice and equal terms, and its positive half covers precarity. A government can't ensure play or affiliation, only leave room for them, through community access and the time and attention that lower precarity frees up
